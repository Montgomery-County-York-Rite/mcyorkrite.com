The Passing Order of St. Paul, historically also known as the
Mediterranean Pass, is a *passing* order: a short ceremony conferred
on the way to a principal order, in the manner of a gate passed while
traveling. In the American York Rite it stands between the
[Illustrious Order of the Red Cross](/about/degrees/illustrious-order-of-the-red-cross/)
and the [Order of Malta](/about/degrees/order-of-malta/), and its
imagery sets the candidate on the sea-road of the Mediterranean, the
world of St. Paul's voyages, storms, and shipwrecks on the journey
toward Rome.

## History

The Malta line of the American Templar system took shape in the early
nineteenth century, as the Commanderies organized their series of
orders. The pathway honored the historical relationship between the
Orders of the Temple and of St. John (the Hospitallers), and it
required a ceremony of passing before the Order of Malta could be
conferred. The Mediterranean Pass, under the invocation of St. Paul,
whose voyages across that same sea the Book of Acts records, served
that purpose, and the passing order carrying his name remained part of
the system as the Grand Encampment standardized the American series.

As a "passing" rather than a principal order, it is deliberately
compact; its presence in the Commandery preserves a very old piece of
Masonic architecture, in which progress through the orders is imagined
as a pilgrimage with its stations along the way.

## In the York Rite

The Passing Order of St. Paul is conferred within the
[Commandery](/about/commandery/), between the
[Illustrious Order of the Red Cross](/about/degrees/illustrious-order-of-the-red-cross/)
and the [Order of Malta](/about/degrees/order-of-malta/), before the
crowning [Order of the Temple](/about/degrees/order-of-the-temple/).
